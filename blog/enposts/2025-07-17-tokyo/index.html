Tokyo Travelogue

July 17, 2025

I will open a thread here to record my trip to Tokyo.

Day 0

I bought a ticket to Tokyo for July 4, and the reason for that particular date was simply that the ticket was cheapest then. Only after I landed in Tokyo did I learn that the price was so low because of a rumor that a large earthquake would strike Japan on July 5. As we now know, that event never happened, so in the end I came out ahead.

Compared with a trip inside China, there were a few extra things to bring:

  • a passport
  • a visa
  • a data SIM card
  • foreign cash

None of the phones we use at home support eSIM, which makes things a little inconvenient, since a physical card is required. I had heard that the needed card could be bought right at the airport, but to be safe I still bought a SoftBank data card on Taobao beforehand. It cannot make calls, only connect to the internet, but that was already enough for me.

I arrived at Narita Airport a little after seven in the evening. I had expected to board the subway right away, but there were still some immigration procedures to go through. During customs quarantine, a very cute beagle sniffed my luggage — truly adorable. I wanted very much to take a photo, but a sign nearby read “No photography,” so I had to let it go.

According to my earlier plan, I needed to buy a Suica transit card at the airport. Unfortunately, by the time I came out, the JR shop had already closed, so I could only buy a ticket with cash.

The line from Narita Airport into the city is the Keisei Line, heading to Nippori Station, where one changes to another line. I had originally intended to take the Skyliner, but I had not expected the complexity of Tokyo’s railway lines. Unlike the subways and light rail lines back home, Tokyo’s commuter railways are often divided into “limited express,” “express,” and “local” trains, somewhat like the “Z direct,” “K express,” and ordinary trains that stop at every small station on the green trains back home. At first I boarded a local train, and after riding for a while I realized something was wrong, so I got off at a small platform and began studying the logic of Tokyo’s railway system carefully. It happened to be Friday evening, and the platform was full of noisy office workers and students heading out to enjoy themselves, while I stood there in a cold sweat trying to figure out whether this really was the right line and whether I had boarded the wrong train. That was the most nervous moment of the whole trip.

Fortunately, I had gotten off at the right place, and an express train happened to stop at that very platform, so I managed to reach Nippori after all.

At Nippori I could change to the JR line, and JR is also the company that runs Suica, the most commonly used transit card in Tokyo, so I bought a Suica card from a vending machine at the station. After that, everything went smoothly.

By the time I came out of the station it was already past eleven at night. Public safety in Tokyo still feels very good: not far from the subway exit stood an elementary school, there were many surveillance cameras nearby, and along the way I saw a very typical Japanese street: many single-family houses and narrow alleys, somewhat like an urban village back home, yet clearly laid out with order.

I am very grateful to P for hosting me and offering free lodging in Tokyo.

Since the journey had left me exhausted, I went to bed early. That was more or less how the trip began.

Day 1

Today P took me around Tokyo. We mainly stayed around Ueno Park. At noon we had tonkatsu rice near P’s workplace, then passed by a Pokémon vending machine, where I bought a Sprigatito charm. Since the weather was quite hot, our first stop in the afternoon was the indoor Tokyo National Museum.

Although the Tokyo National Museum seems to be the largest national museum in Japan, the collection in its main hall did not strike me as particularly impressive; aside from the two imposing Kongōrikishi statues near the entrance, little left much of an impression on me. The Toyokan gallery on the right side, however, held quite a few distinctive pieces, including many Chinese cultural relics, and even ancient artifacts from Egypt and Mesopotamia. As for how these relics were acquired, the record is mostly murky — the museum craftily labels the provenance as things like “such-and-such expedition,” without any mention of theft or looting. The lost heads of Buddha statues from the Yungang Grottoes in Shanxi are kept here too, and I do not know whether there is still any chance of them being returned to their original place.

Unfortunately, the strap of my Sprigatito charm broke at some point, and I lost it inside the Tokyo National Museum, leaving me with only a photograph — quite a shame.

After leaving the museum, I wandered a bit around Ueno Park and reached Shinobazu Pond, a small pond covered with lotus flowers. Such a small pond has little of its own character; the small ponds in the center of Kunming feel just about the same.

The University of Tokyo campus is nearby, so I went to have a look as well, and took a few photos in front of the famous Yasuda Auditorium. The buildings on the campus of the University of Tokyo are quite distinctive, all in a Victorian style — they would not look out of place in a fantasy film either.

After having curry near the University of Tokyo in the evening, we went to walk around Akihabara. Although it is called an “otaku holy land,” since I am no longer much of an otaku myself, it did not move me very much. We went into a few shops selling anime-themed goods, and they felt extremely similar to the otaku malls near West Lake — I suspect the commercial ecosystem around West Lake was in fact modeled on Akihabara. Right-wing politics in Japan seems to be on the rise lately; there was a right-wing politician giving a street speech, and quite a few people were cheering him on. In the end I bought two comics in Akihabara — the first volume of Berserk and the first volume of Delicious in Dungeon — to keep as souvenirs.

Akihabara has many gambling parlors. I stopped by a slot machine and pachinko shop to watch young Japanese people gamble, and it seemed to me that they simply sat there pressing the buttons mechanically, nonstop, much like mice in a Skinner box — their state of mind looked quite worrying. Some of the slot machines even used Madoka Magica as a theme, which, as a fan of Madoka, left me rather annoyed.

That was the whole of that day.

Day 2

From this day on, I explored Tokyo by myself.

The first stop of the day was Shibuya. Shibuya is really just a large shopping district; anyone who enjoys shopping would probably be delighted here, but since I do not have much interest in shopping myself, I simply wandered around without any particular purpose.

Right outside Shibuya Station stands the famous statue of Hachikō, the loyal dog. Many people line up here to take photos with the statue. Many others come to photograph the diagonal crossing.

The shopping centers here often devote an entire building to a single brand — both IKEA and MUJI work this way. There was not much worth mentioning about either, though while visiting IKEA I had lunch at its restaurant, and at MUJI I bought a Hello Kitty refrigerator magnet.

In the afternoon I went to Shinjuku Gyoen, which reminded me a little of places like the Summer Palace or Yuyuantan back home. Even though it was a Sunday, the park was not very crowded, so I could lie leisurely on the great lawn and gaze at the sky. Makoto Shinkai’s film The Garden of Words was also filmed here, though I have actually never watched that film, so I did not fully appreciate the reference. Still, the scenery really was quite beautiful. There is also a famous staircase from Your Name nearby, but touring Shinjuku Gyoen had already worn me out, so I did not go there.

On the way back in the evening, I passed through Shibuya again, thinking of going up the Skytree there to watch the sunset. The tickets, however, had already sold out, so that did not happen. I then dropped by the Pokémon Center there, hoping to buy another Sprigatito charm, but I could not find one — it had sold out because it was so popular, which was rather disappointing.

Being both tired and hungry, I had no mood to look for anything special, so I settled for McDonald’s, filled my stomach, and went back.

That was the second day.

Day 3

The plan for this day was to visit Kamakura. Kamakura lies south of Tokyo, farther than Yokohama, but thanks to Tokyo’s convenient commuter rail system, and the fact that where I was staying was already near the southern edge of the city, it took only about an hour and a half to get there.

At JR Kamakura Station, one can buy a ticket for the sightseeing tram Enoden for 800 yen, valid for unlimited rides that day. The tram sets off from Kamakura, and at first the scenery is unremarkable — just ordinary Japanese streets — but then, quite suddenly, the tram reaches the coast, and the view opens up completely. My first stop was Shichirigahama, where there is a very nice beach said to have appeared as a setting in many anime. I heard that on clear days one can see Mount Fuji rising above the sea from here, but the humidity was high that day, and visibility was poor, so I could not actually see it.

The second stop was Kamakurakoukou-mae. There is a level crossing here facing the sea, and because it appeared in Slam Dunk, many people come to take photographs. The sea, the railway, and the tram together do have a distinctive charm; it is no wonder that a few years ago, so many so-called “mini Kamakura” spots turned into online tourist attractions back home.

The third stop was Enoshima. There is a small hill on the island with many shrines and other historic sites, but since I am not very familiar with Japanese history, I could not really find much interest in this particular attraction. The path up the hill was lined with many small food stalls, mostly serving seafood.

By the time I came down the hill it was already three or four in the afternoon, so I decided to head back and take a look around Kamakura itself. The streets of Kamakura feel somewhat like a commercial old town back home, with many shops catering to tourists, and a lively atmosphere throughout. The center of Kamakura is a shrine, Tsurugaoka Hachiman-gū, located in Yukinoshita. I heard that this shrine originally stood in Yuigahama, two tram stops away, and was later moved here by Minamoto no Yoritomo. Japan celebrates Tanabata according to the solar calendar, so the shrine was holding its Tanabata Festival, and I could see shrine maidens dressed in red and white preparing for the event.

By the time I finished looking around it was nearly dark, so I turned back and returned to Tokyo.

Day 5

I skipped Day 4. I had originally planned to go to Lake Kawaguchi that day to see Mount Fuji, but after checking the weather forecast and a live camera, I found that Mount Fuji had been shrouded in cloud for several days in a row, which left me quite unmotivated, so I simply stayed in my room for the day, treating it as a rest day as well.

The first stop on the fifth day was Sensō-ji, a temple with strongly Japanese architecture. I drew a fortune slip at Sensō-ji, and unfortunately it turned out to be a bad one, warning of poorly handled relationships, flowers that bloom too late, and even a ghost coming to steal money, among other things. I then tied the slip to the rack beside the temple and left in a rather deflated mood.

The second stop was Ginza, somewhat like Wangfujing or Nanjing Road back home, full of shops that looked far too luxurious for me to even enter. At the Ginza intersection stands the building that appeared in Apocalypse Hotel, a currently very popular anime. The Imperial Palace is also near Ginza, but since I had not made a reservation, I could not go in. This should be the most central part of Tokyo, yet, rather surprisingly, homeless people and beggars often nap in the bushes here.

The third stop was Tokyo Tower. Seen from a distance, Tokyo Tower does not leave much of an impression — it simply looks like a fairly attractive shade of red. Looking up from directly beneath the tower, though, gives quite a striking visual effect. A ticket lets you go up to the top, from where you can look out over Tokyo Bay; Tokyo, Kawasaki, Yokohama, and Yokosuka can all be seen from up there, and the view is really quite beautiful.

By the time I came down from Tokyo Tower it was nearly dark. That was the fifth day.

Day 6

The destination that day was Yokosuka, the very place where American forces landed after Japan’s surrender at the end of the Second World War. Today it serves as a naval base for both the US Navy and the Japan Maritime Self-Defense Force. The headquarters of the US Seventh Fleet is also located here.

After taking the JR Yokosuka Line to Yokosuka Station, one finds a waterfront park right outside, which also happens to be an excellent spot to look out over the naval port. When I visited, there was, unfortunately, no aircraft carrier in port, but there was a US Navy Zumwalt-class destroyer, whose shape looks remarkably futuristic — a design meant for electromagnetic stealth. According to some military enthusiasts, though, the actual performance of this ship is not very impressive. On the other side of the port sat some “small boats” of the Japan Maritime Self-Defense Force — vessels of a more traditional appearance, with tall masts.

Following the park further, one reaches a shopping mall. Inside, you can buy tickets for a “warship cruise,” and from the pier behind the mall a tour boat takes you close to the warships inside the port. The full loop around the port takes about forty-five minutes, and there is a guide on board who explains each ship in the harbor — though my Japanese is far too weak, and I could only catch the occasional katakana English word here and there, with no real idea of what he was saying.

Walking east along the road from the mall gives a good look at the streets of Yokosuka, where shops flying American flags are everywhere and the signboards are almost all in English — presumably to serve the American servicemen stationed at the base here. At the end of the road is Mikasa Park, where the flagship of the Japanese Combined Fleet during the Russo-Japanese War, the Mikasa, is docked. Here one can sense a distinctly right-wing atmosphere: the park holds a statue of Admiral Tōgō Heihachirō of the Imperial Japanese Navy from that era, along with slogans such as “the rise or fall of the empire depends on this one battle.” As a piece of history, I think the ship is worth going aboard to see, though it is filled with facilities for Japanese right-wing patriotic education, and the Russo-Japanese War is even framed there as a “war of liberation for the peoples of Asia,” which felt rather uncomfortable to witness. I imagine the character “Mikasa” in Attack on Titan probably takes her name from this very ship.

This is the final entry of the Tokyo travelogue. On Friday I simply wandered around Ikebukuro without finding anything particularly interesting worth writing about, so I will end the blog here.
